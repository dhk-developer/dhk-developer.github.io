Background

Memora is a rhythm game in the style of Phigros and Arcaea, which I started in late 2025. In those games the lines you tap on move, spin and disappear as the song plays, and I wanted to take that idea further, so in Memora a note can also float free of any line, fly towards another note, or aim at a point on the screen that is still moving when you hit it. There's a visual-novel story mode as well. The game targets Android and I'm building it in Unity 6 with C#, on my own, in the evenings and at weekends.

I'm keeping the source code private for now because I'd like to release the game at some point. If you want to look at more of the engineering, I've written up the architecture with some diagrams, and put a small separate code sample, in a public repository on GitHub.

Keeping judgement apart from the visuals

The first real problem came up as soon as the lines started moving, because it was no longer obvious where and when a note should be judged. In my early prototype the note's animation and its hit position were stored together, which meant that any visual flourish I added also changed the gameplay without my meaning it to. If an entrance animation overshot, the hit point overshot with it, and a bit of camera shake was enough to move a target away from where the player expected it.

I got round this by giving every playable note two separate sets of data. One set holds the things that decide the score: the beat it falls on, whatever it's aimed at, and whether it's a tap, a hold, a slide or a flick. The other set describes how the note looks on its way in and out, written as keyframes measured in beats so that the movement can follow the music. Input is only ever compared against the first set, and the camera, layers and effects are applied after the hit position has already been worked out, so none of them can drag a target somewhere else. Having the two apart also let me add notes that are purely decorative, which look exactly like the real thing but are never judged, and they turned out to be useful for fake-outs in the choreography.

It does mean more data per note, and I have two code paths that have to stay consistent with each other. I think that's a fair price, because when a play comes back as a Miss I only need to look in one place to find out why.

Timing

Counting frames isn't accurate enough for a rhythm game, and over the length of a song it slowly drifts away from the audio, so Memora takes its time from Unity's audio (DSP) clock. The song is scheduled to start at a known point on that clock, and the current song position is the audio time since then, minus the chart's own offset and whatever latency has been measured for the player's device. Every line position, target and animation is then calculated from that beat on each frame, instead of being moved a little at a time, which means a dropped frame can't push anything off course.

Scores are out of 1,000,000. Accuracy is worth 900,000 of that, with a Great counting for 70% of a Perfect, and the other 100,000 comes from combo.

Try it yourself

This is a stripped-down version of how Memora judges a tap. Once you press Start, a ring will close in on the circle at 100 beats per minute, and you tap the circle (or press Space) at the moment the ring meets it. Each tap is graded with the same windows the game uses by default, so anything within 100 ms of the beat counts as a Perfect, anything within 250 ms is a Great, and the rest are misses.

Like the game, the demo keeps time using the audio clock instead of counting frames. If your taps keep landing late even when they feel right, that's probably your browser or headphones adding a little delay of their own, and measuring that delay is what the calibration screen in Memora is for.

Phones and headphones all add some delay of their own, which is why the game has a calibration screen. It plays a looping four-beat bar and asks you to tap along four times, and I take the average of the middle two offsets, so one badly mistimed tap can't drag the result too far. If the taps are spread out too much, it asks you to try again instead of saving a number it can't trust. Before writing any of this I wrote the rules out as plain sentences, which is something I picked up from writing acceptance criteria at work, and having them in front of me made the code much easier to get right.

Notes that aim at moving things

Letting one note aim at another raised a question I hadn't thought about at first, which is at what moment the game should look at the target's position. Say note B is flying towards note A. B needs to arrive where A will be at the moment B is hit, which isn't necessarily where A was when A itself was hit, so the target has to be sampled at B's hit time.

To make that dependable, anything a note can aim at has to be able to work out its position from the chart, the beat and the size of the screen and nothing else, so it isn't allowed to rely on the previous frame or on anything remembered during play. That constraint is what allows the editor to jump backwards to any point in a song and draw exactly what it would have drawn if you'd played up to that point, and I have automated checks that seek backwards and compare the two.

There are two ways a note can approach its target. By default it heads for where the target will be when you hit it, which is the easiest to read. The other option follows the target as it moves, and has a correction for lines that jump instantly from one place to another, so that the note doesn't jump along with the line.

The charting editor

Charts are stored as JSON, and writing something this choreographed by hand was never going to be realistic, so I built an editor window inside Unity for making them. It has a timeline, a live preview of the playfield, a waveform view and slowed-down playback, layers, a library of reusable patterns, and undo.

For a while the editor preview had its own simplified copy of the gameplay maths, and the two gradually drifted apart until the preview no longer matched what you'd see in the game. I fixed that by moving the maths into shared code that both the game and the editor call, and I added checks that compare their output. If I were starting again I'd write that shared code first.

Being able to jump straight to any beat took a few specific pieces of work. The editor keeps an interval tree so it can find everything that's active at a given beat without scanning the whole chart. Repeated events are stored once and only expanded for the stretch of the song being looked at, and a cached running total converts changes in scroll speed into distance. When you scrub quickly, each new seek request replaces any older one that hasn't finished, and the preview waits until a result is complete before drawing it.

Tracking down a slowdown in the editor

Once charts got bigger, playback in the editor started to stutter, and I profiled it before changing anything. The culprit was one of the inspector panels, which was serialising the entire chart four times on every layout pass and another four times on every repaint, just to get ready for an undo that nobody had asked for.

I changed the panel so that it only records undo when something is edited, cached a few results that were being recalculated several times per repaint, and stopped drawing timeline rows that were off the screen. I ran 18,125 automated comparisons between cached and freshly calculated results, compared screenshots taken at the same beats (they came out pixel-identical), and checked the hash of every chart file to make sure none of them had been modified along the way.

Reusable patterns

Charts tend to repeat ideas, such as a stream of notes along a curve or two lines passing a melody back and forth, so I save those as presets with a few adjustable parameters. When you apply a preset it's copied into the chart as ordinary notes with new IDs, all as a single undo step, and the chart records which version of the preset it came from. I went with copies over live links because I didn't want an edit to a preset to change songs I'd already finished without me noticing.

Keeping old charts and saves working

New features are added as optional data, and when something replaces an older feature, the old fields are still read even though nothing writes to them any more. Before the editor saves a chart, it checks for anything the game can't handle yet, so that nothing gets dropped without warning.

Story progress is saved with a checksum, written to a temporary file first and then swapped into place with a backup kept alongside, so a crash halfway through saving can't corrupt it. The game will also refuse to overwrite a save that came from a newer version of itself.

What isn't finished

Songs that change tempo partway through aren't supported yet. Doing that properly means having one clock that playback, the grid, the waveform and judgement all agree on, and I'd prefer to leave the feature out for now than ship a version that only approximately works. Layers on playable notes are reserved in the data format but aren't hooked up. I also haven't done proper performance testing across a range of phones; the editor figures above are measured, and measuring on real devices is the next thing on my list.

What I'd do differently

I'd split the project into separate assemblies early on. Two of the classes grew very large, and although breaking them into partial files made them easier to read, it didn't untangle them. I'd also write the shared gameplay maths before building the editor preview, so that the two could never drift, and I'd start testing on real phones much sooner than I did.

If you'd like to talk about any of this, the parts I most enjoy explaining are the audio clock, the notes that aim at moving targets, and how I checked that the editor fix didn't change anything.
